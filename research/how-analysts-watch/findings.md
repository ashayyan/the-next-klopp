# How analysts watch: findings

## Key concepts

- Analysts watch with a question already chosen, such as a player, a pattern, or one phase of play, instead of following the ball. [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch][src-asa-day-in-life-analyst]
- Open play splits into four phases: attacking, defending, and the two transitions. Naming the phase of each moment is the first analyst habit. [src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst]
- Analyst work has three stages: live, post-match, and observation by technical observers who pair what they see with data. [src-uefa-performance-analysis]
- The tactical feed is a fixed wide shot from the top of the stand, built to keep the twenty outfield players in view. [src-arxiv-crang-broadcast-tracking#tactical-feed-20-outfield]
- The programme feed is what viewers see: cuts between angles, graphics and replays. It follows the ball, so off-ball players are often out of frame. [src-arxiv-crang-broadcast-tracking][src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst]
- Perceptual-cognitive skill means how well a player picks up cues and predicts what comes next. The anticipation evidence is stronger than the scanning evidence. [src-frontiers-cognition-elite-soccer]

## Must teach

### Viewing methods (what to watch)

- Follow one player for a spell. Examples are a full-back who steps into midfield, or a defensive midfielder while his team attacks. [src-breakingthelines-watch-space]
- Follow one unit or moment: the centre-backs right after a turnover, or the striker while the ball is on the far wing. [src-breakingthelines-watch-space]
- Follow one phase: split the game into defensive, middle and attacking thirds and study each in and out of possession. [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]
- Watch one team at a time, because following both means missing the off-ball play. [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]
- Watch in blocks by phase and zone, using three defensive blocks (low, mid, high) and three attacking phases (build-up, progression, creation). [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]
- Researchers cut matches into fixed quarter-hour intervals. Football blocks by time are a research convention, and the sources found give no analyst rule for them. [src-jssm-linke-effective-playing-time]
- First watch for the story, then rewatch to study tactics, pausing and rewinding. [src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst]
- Mute the commentary so pundits do not shape what you notice. [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]

### Cameras, notes and condensed viewing

- The wide tactical feed shows shape and spacing. The tighter coach-preferred or broadcast view shows detail. One analyst codes from the tighter view live, then stacks both views after the match. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst]
- Take notes in columns by phase, or two pages titled in and out of possession, with short codes such as POFB for playing out from the back. [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]
- Coding windows with hotkeys let an analyst cut each repetition or moment into a tagged clip. The code set follows the team's game model. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst]
- Ball-in-play time falls over a match, from 66.3% of total time in the first quarter-hour to 55.9% in the last. A full 90 contains much dead time. [src-jssm-linke-effective-playing-time#effective-time-decline]
- Analysts routinely turn a match into short clips: half-time clips for the dressing room and a detailed code afterwards. [src-worldrugby-halftime-analysis][src-worldrugby-postmatch-analysis]

### Professional habits

- Before kickoff an analyst builds the opposition report from video and data, and picks metrics for this opponent. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst]
- During the match, live coding collects the main tactical themes. If live coding is impossible, a manual tally of key events still works. [src-worldrugby-halftime-analysis]
- Half-time asks what the tactical trend is, what has worked and what has not. [src-worldrugby-halftime-analysis]
- Right after the game comes a short hot debrief against the game model, then a fuller coded review with footage. [src-worldrugby-postmatch-analysis]
- Football analysis here is a team job. UEFA's technical observers describe their role as analysing, not criticising people. [src-uefa-performance-analysis]
- The full video-analysis workflow belongs to a later track. Here only the watching habits matter. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst]

### Evidence on expert perception

- Anticipation is the strongest finding. Skilled players predict opponents' actions better and faster, using posture, context and structure. [src-frontiers-cognition-elite-soccer]
- Scanning is weaker. Eye-tracking meta-analysis across 22 team-sport studies found no clear expert-novice difference in fixation count, fixation length or quiet eye. Heterogeneity was very high. [src-frontiers-visual-search-meta]
- Skilled players adapt their scanning to the situation, so no single scanning style marks an expert. [src-frontiers-cognition-elite-soccer]
- Evidence strength: most of this is laboratory work on players, not on spectators learning to watch. Off-ball play, most of a match, is barely studied. [src-frontiers-cognition-elite-soccer]
- Video-based training in football shows benefit for anticipation and decision-making, but only 10 small studies of moderate quality, and one had a transfer test. [src-frontiers-vbt-football-review]
- Temporal occlusion clips (a clip frozen before the key action, then a prediction) gave a large pooled gain across 12 intervention studies, including on field tests. [src-springer-temporal-occlusion-meta]
- Broader perceptual-cognitive training transferred to on-court performance with a medium effect, weaker than the large lab effect. Study quality was weak. [src-mdpi-pct-team-sports-meta]

### Rebuilding attention: football practice versus general attention research

- Football-analyst practice (watch with a question) and general learning research (questions before a video lower mind wandering) point the same way. They are separate bodies of evidence. [src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch][src-jarmac-pan-pretesting-video#pretest-reduced-mw]
- Pretest experiments used one 26-minute recorded lecture, with questions between parts or all at the start, and both lowered mind wandering and raised final-test scores. [src-jarmac-pan-pretesting-video#pretest-26min-lecture][src-jarmac-pan-pretesting-video#pretest-reduced-mw]
- Large-scale video data shows median engagement stayed at or below six minutes, whatever the total video length. [src-acm-guo-mooc-video-engagement#median-engagement-6min]
- The same authors advise cutting instructional video into chunks of less than six minutes. [src-acm-guo-mooc-video-engagement#segment-under-6min]
- The popular 10 to 15 minute attention limit rests on thin evidence, so the learner should control session length instead of a fixed rule. [src-aps-bradbury-attention-span]

### Watch Gym Level 1 inputs

- Use one question per clip, asked before the clip plays. Support: pretests reduced mind wandering and match analysts pick a focus first. [src-jarmac-pan-pretesting-video#pretest-reduced-mw][src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]
- Keep every clip far shorter than six minutes and end the session before engagement drops, with the learner choosing to lengthen. [src-acm-guo-mooc-video-engagement#segment-under-6min][src-aps-bradbury-attention-span]
- Question types that fit the evidence: where is the space, who presses first, which shape, and what happens next (an occlusion pause). [src-springer-temporal-occlusion-meta][src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst]
- Prefer a wide, full-pitch view for shape and spacing questions, since broadcast cuts hide off-ball players. [src-arxiv-crang-broadcast-tracking#tactical-feed-20-outfield][src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst]
- Answer on a pitch panel by tapping where the space is, a sport-specific response. The evidence for that format over verbal answers is not significant. [src-mdpi-pct-team-sports-meta]
- Spotlight exercises give the first clips: watch only the left-back, watch the centre-backs after a turnover. [src-breakingthelines-watch-space]

## Misconceptions

- "Watch the ball." Analysts deliberately keep part of their attention on off-ball players and the shape. [src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst][src-breakingthelines-watch-space]
- "You must watch all 90 minutes to learn." Analysts cut matches into coded clips, and ball-in-play time shrinks as a match goes on. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst][src-jssm-linke-effective-playing-time#effective-time-decline]
- "Experts simply look in more places." Eye-tracking differences between experts and novices were not clear in the meta-analysis. [src-frontiers-visual-search-meta][src-frontiers-cognition-elite-soccer]
- "Attention always fails after a quarter of an hour." That claim rests mostly on repeated citation. [src-aps-bradbury-attention-span]
- "Screen training makes you better in matches." Transfer is real in the pooled studies but smaller than in the lab and hard to trust given weak study quality. [src-mdpi-pct-team-sports-meta][src-frontiers-vbt-football-review]
- "A formation label tells you what the team is doing." Teams change shape by phase, so label the phase first. [src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst][src-mastermindsite-how-i-watch]

## Contested points

Contested: broadcast footage versus the wide tactical camera. One writer says standard broadcast cameras miss much of the pitch and recommends the wide view or live viewing, and the tactical feed is designed to keep the outfield players in shot. [src-fourfourtwo-watch-like-analyst][src-arxiv-crang-broadcast-tracking#tactical-feed-20-outfield] An analyst's coaches, however, prefer a more zoomed-in view on matchdays, and he combines both views afterwards, so neither wins outright. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst]

Contested: whether to trust the eye or the numbers. One analyst checks raw numbers for the opponent and picks metrics for it, while a popular piece warns that possession or pass completion mislead without watching the play. [src-asa-day-in-life-analyst][src-breakingthelines-watch-space]

Contested: how long a session can be. Large-scale video data shows engagement time falling, with a six-minute guideline, while another author says the fixed 10 to 15 minute rule has little primary support. [src-acm-guo-mooc-video-engagement#median-engagement-6min][src-aps-bradbury-attention-span]

Contested: whether expert perception can be trained from video. Pooled studies show gains in anticipation, with weaker transfer and weak study quality, and the football-specific review found little evidence of real-match or retained benefit. [src-springer-temporal-occlusion-meta][src-mdpi-pct-team-sports-meta][src-frontiers-vbt-football-review]

## Open questions

- Clip length for Level 1 is a design choice. Evidence only bounds it (far under six minutes, learner-controlled), so pilot tens of seconds to a couple of minutes and let the learner extend.
- Which question types does the learner enjoy? The research supports prediction and spotlight prompts, but no source ranks them, so test them.
- Does a tap on a pitch panel beat a multiple-choice answer for this learner? No direct evidence was found.
- Is there an analyst source on watching in time blocks (for example by quarter-hour)? None was found beyond research conventions.
- Which licensed footage can the app show? YouTube clips are link-only because automated access and download are not allowed. [src-youtube-terms]
- Does a first-person or tactical view beat broadcast footage for a spectator learning shape? The football review notes mixed viewpoints but no clean comparison. [src-frontiers-vbt-football-review]
