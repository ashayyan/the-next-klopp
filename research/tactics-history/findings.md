# Tactics history: findings

## Key concepts

- Law changes steer tactics: the 1925 offside cut and the 1990 "level is onside" change both made a defensive line harder to hold and helped attackers. [src-ifab-history-offside-law#offside-1925-three-to-two][src-ifab-history-offside-law#offside-1990-level-is-onside]
- Rules can also react to tactics: the back-pass ban was trialled in 1991 and adopted in 1992 against time-wasting. [src-ifab-history-back-pass-law#backpass-1992-introduced]
- Passing versus dribbling: the pack-dribbling game gave way to combined play, helped by looser offside rules outside London. [src-whiterose-evolution-football-passing]
- Formation numbers are labels added later: the 2-3-5 was named the Pyramid and numbered in hindsight, and the 4-2-4 was the first formation described by numbers. [src-wikipedia-formation-association-football]
- Pressing runs through the story: aggressive pressing spread from the 1960s, and "gegenpressing" is a later German coaching label. [src-premier-league-counter-pressing-explained]
- Compact lines and zonal marking replaced man-marking and the sweeper in Sacchi's Milan. [src-uefa-ac-milan-1988-90]
- The data thread: Opta's expected goals (xG) model reached fans and clubs from 2012. [src-theanalyst-what-is-xg]

## Must teach

- 1863: The new FA in London wrote the first widely recognised Laws of Association Football; the IFAB followed in 1886. Every later tactic answers these rules. [src-ifab-history-origins-modern-game#fa-1863-first-laws]
- 1860s-70s: Passing and combination play replaced lone dribbling, with Sheffield and Scotland using a looser offside rule than London. [src-whiterose-evolution-football-passing]
- 1890s: The 2-3-5 Pyramid became the English standard and lasted to the 1930s (low confidence, secondary source). [src-wikipedia-formation-association-football]
- 1925: Offside needed two opponents, not three, between attacker and goal line, and the IFAB says this pushed teams to new attacking and defensive systems. [src-ifab-history-offside-law#offside-1925-three-to-two]
- 1925: Chapman's Arsenal answered with a deeper centre-half, the WM (3-2-2-3), by the club's account after a 7-0 defeat at Newcastle. See Contested. [src-arsenal-newcastle-7-0-golden-era][src-wikipedia-formation-association-football]
- 1932: Rappan's verrou at Servette, a modified 2-3-5 with a spare defender, is the usual root of catenaccio. See Contested. [src-wikipedia-catenaccio]
- 1953: Hungary beat England 6-3 at Wembley, the first home defeat by a side from outside the British Isles; Hidegkuti, a deep-lying centre-forward, pulled defenders out of place. [src-uefa-hungary-1950-56]
- 1958: Brazil won the World Cup with the 4-2-4, a reaction to the WM's stiffness (low confidence, one secondary source). [src-wikipedia-formation-association-football]
- 1971: Ajax won the first of three straight European Cups; Michels's Total Football was a 4-3-3 with pressing and an offside trap. [src-uefa-ajax-1971-73]
- 1987: Sacchi's Milan played a high-line 4-4-2 with zonal marking and an offside trap, and won the 1989 and 1990 European Cups. [src-uefa-ac-milan-1988-90]
- 1992 (PL): Law change: keepers may not handle deliberate back-passes, a ban made for the 1992/93 season. [src-ifab-history-back-pass-law#backpass-1992-introduced]
- 1992 (PL): The 22 First Division clubs left the Football League, formed the Premier League company in May and opened the first season on 15 August, with a BSkyB television deal. [src-premier-league-history]
- 1996 (PL): Wenger joined Arsenal; the league credits his ideas with changing English football on and off the pitch, and the 2003/04 side went unbeaten. [src-premier-league-hall-of-fame-wenger]
- 2004 (PL): Mourinho's Chelsea moved from a diamond 4-4-2 to a 4-3-3 and won with 95 points, conceding only 15 league goals. [src-premier-league-chelsea-2004-05#fifteen-goals-conceded]
- 2008: Guardiola's Barcelona mixed possession with high pressing and won six trophies in year one; he named Cruyff and Rexach as the source. [src-uefa-barcelona-2008-12]
- 2010: Klopp's Dortmund won the Bundesliga in 2010/11 and 2011/12 with "heavy-metal" gegenpressing. [src-bundesliga-klopp-dortmund-years]
- 2012: Opta's Sam Green introduced expected goals to a wide audience. See Contested. [src-theanalyst-what-is-xg]
- 2015 (PL): Leicester won from a compact 4-4-2 built on transitions, with the lowest possession of any champion since 2003/04. [src-premier-league-leicester-2015-16#lowest-possession-champion]
- 2017 (PL): Guardiola's City won the league with 100 points after tucking full-backs into midfield and using the keeper as a passer. [src-premier-league-man-city-evolution]
- 2019 (PL): Klopp's Liverpool won the title after defence and press were rebuilt, conceding the fewest league goals. [src-premier-league-klopp-liverpool-2019-20]

## Misconceptions

- "Tactics began in the 2000s": passing play and positions were already being reported in 1860s Sheffield. [src-whiterose-evolution-football-passing]
- "The 4-4-2 is English by nature": Sacchi's Italian Milan used it with zonal marking, and Ranieri's Leicester used it as a counter-attack shape. [src-uefa-ac-milan-1988-90][src-premier-league-leicester-2015-16]
- "Catenaccio was Herrera's invention": the page treats him as the popular face of a system others built earlier. [src-wikipedia-catenaccio]
- "Gegenpressing is Klopp's invention": pressing was adopted from the 1960s and the German coaches behind the term include Frank, Rangnick and Klopp. [src-premier-league-counter-pressing-explained]
- "England 1966 were wingless wonders in a 4-4-2": one account calls this a cliche and describes a 4-1-3-2 with Stiles as anchor. [src-fourfourtwo-england-1966-formation]

## Contested points

Contested: Who invented pressing? One account says no single inventor exists, weighing Maslov at Dynamo Kyiv, Happel's Feyenoord, Michels and Sacchi, and traces forechecking to ice hockey. [src-fourfourtwo-who-invented-pressing] The Premier League explainer instead lists Maslov, Happel, Michels, Lobanovskyi and Sacchi together as the 1960s-onward adopters. [src-premier-league-counter-pressing-explained]

Contested: Who created catenaccio? The Wikipedia entry names Rappan's 1932 verrou first, then Italian precursors (Villini 1941, Barbieri 1944-46, Viani 1948) with Rocco's 1947 version as the "real" one, and Herrera as popularizer. [src-wikipedia-catenaccio] A 90min profile credits Rocco as pioneer but says the Swiss-to-Italian route is only "purported". [src-90min-nereo-rocco]

Contested: Did Chapman invent the WM after Newcastle? The club account says the defeat and the 1925 offside change led to it, partly on Buchan's memoir. [src-arsenal-newcastle-7-0-golden-era] A club-history blog says the story is not clean: formations varied and forwards were already dropping back in central Europe. [src-aisa-chapman-new-formation-1925]

Contested: Who gave football the passing game and the Pyramid? A 2025 peer-reviewed paper challenges the claim that Scotland alone pioneered passing, citing 1860s Sheffield reports. [src-whiterose-evolution-football-passing] Wikipedia gives rival dates for the first 2-3-5 (a claim for Wrexham in 1877, another for 1880) and does not settle them. [src-wikipedia-formation-association-football]

Contested: Where did xG begin? Stats Perform says Opta's Sam Green introduced it in 2012. [src-theanalyst-what-is-xg] These Football Times traces the term to 1993 and Reep and Benjamin's 1960s shot studies. [src-thesefootballtimes-roots-of-xg]
